# S008 — Distributed Practice in Verbal Recall Tasks

## Why it matters

Spacing is foundational for a learning engine that can choose not only what comes next but when to revisit it.

## Review and findings

The quantitative synthesis covers 317 experiments reported in 184 articles. Distributed practice generally improves later verbal recall relative to massed practice. The effective gap between study events depends on the desired retention interval; there is no context-free optimal spacing schedule.

## Limitations and boundary conditions

The review concentrates on verbal recall. The relationship between spacing and complex skill acquisition, collaborative practice, motivation, and authentic workplace performance needs more targeted evidence. Product scheduling must account for dropout and opportunity costs.

## Evidence profile

| Dimension | Rating | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Internal validity | moderate | Large synthesis of heterogeneous experiments |
| Directness | high | Directly evaluates distributed versus massed practice for recall |
| Consistency | high | Broadly consistent benefit with quantitative moderators |
| Replication | high | Hundreds of experiments |
| Magnitude | moderate | Depends strongly on intervals and task conditions |
| Duration | high | Retention interval is central to the analysis |
| Transfer | low | Primarily verbal recall rather than transfer |
| Ecological validity | low | Many studies use controlled memory tasks |
